Bias, Confounding, and Error

Not every difference or pattern is the effect you think it is. Learn systematic bias, random error, measurement error, and confounding through practical examples.

◷ 50 minBeginner
ObjectivesLearnExampleTry itExercisesSummary
Start here

What you will learn

  • Distinguish bias from random error.
  • Recognize selection, measurement, and response bias.
  • Explain confounding.
  • Design checks that reduce avoidable error.
01

Bias and random error

Bias is a systematic tendency for results to differ from the truth in a particular direction. Random error creates unpredictable variation around the truth. Larger samples usually reduce random sampling error, but they do not automatically remove systematic bias.

02

Sources of bias

Selection bias occurs when included observations differ systematically from those excluded. Measurement bias arises from instruments or definitions. Response bias occurs when answers are influenced by wording, social desirability, memory, or interviewer effects. Survivorship bias ignores cases that disappeared before observation.

03

Confounding

A confounder is associated with both the suspected cause and the outcome, creating or distorting an observed relationship. For example, ice-cream sales and drowning may rise together because hot weather affects both. Association alone does not identify causation.

04

Error is broader than sampling

Processing mistakes, duplicate records, incorrect joins, time-zone conversions, coding changes, and data entry problems can also alter results. Analysts need validation checks at each stage, not only statistical formulas at the end.

Vocabulary

Key terms

Bias

Systematic deviation from the truth or target quantity.

Random error

Unpredictable variation caused by chance processes.

Confounder

A variable related to both an exposure and outcome that distorts their association.

Measurement error

Difference between a recorded value and the intended quantity.

Example

Training and employee performance

Employees who attended optional training have higher performance scores.

  1. 1

    The association is descriptive.

  2. 2

    Training attendance was not randomly assigned.

  3. 3

    More motivated or experienced employees may be more likely to attend.

  4. 4

    Manager support may affect both attendance and performance.

  5. 5

    Compare baseline performance, role, experience, and assignment process before causal claims.

Conclusion

The data support an association, not proof that training caused the difference.

Summary

What to remember

  • Bias is systematic; random error is chance variation.
  • Large samples do not cure systematic bias.
  • Confounding can create misleading associations.
  • Validation must cover the full data workflow.
