Analytical Questions and Defining Metrics

Turn a decision need into answerable questions and precisely defined metrics. Learn grain, numerator, denominator, time window, filters, comparison, and ownership.

◷ 50 minBeginner
ObjectivesLearnExampleTry itExercisesSummary
Start here

What you will learn

  • Translate a decision into analytical questions.
  • Define metrics with complete business logic.
  • Separate outputs, outcomes, and drivers.
  • Identify denominator and grain errors.
01

Question before metric

Begin with the decision and user: who will act, what choice is being made, and what evidence would change the choice? Then define descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, or causal questions. “Build a dashboard” is a deliverable, not an analytical question.

02

Metric specification

A reproducible metric specifies name, purpose, grain, numerator, denominator, inclusion and exclusion rules, time window, unit, source, refresh timing, owner, and known limitations. “Conversion rate” is incomplete until an eligible visitor and conversion event are defined.

03

Output, outcome, and driver

Outputs count activities delivered, outcomes represent desired changes, and drivers are factors believed to influence results. Training sessions delivered is an output; skill improvement is an outcome; attendance and practice may be drivers. Mixing them leads to weak conclusions.

04

Comparisons and context

A metric needs context: target, prior period, benchmark, segment, or control group. A 12% conversion rate may be excellent or poor depending on channel, product, season, and eligibility. Avoid comparisons that change definitions or populations.

Vocabulary

Key terms

Metric

A defined quantitative measure used to monitor or evaluate something.

Grain

The level represented by one row or metric event.

Numerator

The counted or summed quantity above the division line.

Denominator

The eligible base used to calculate a rate or proportion.

Example

Defining an on-time delivery rate

Operations wants to know whether delivery performance improved.

  1. 1

    Define eligible delivered orders and exclude cancelled orders.

  2. 2

    Define on-time: delivered on or before promised timestamp.

  3. 3

    Numerator: eligible orders delivered on time.

  4. 4

    Denominator: all eligible delivered orders.

  5. 5

    Specify timezone, period, order grain, data source, and comparison period.

Conclusion

The metric can now be reproduced and audited instead of interpreted differently by each team.

What to remember

  • Rates require explicit denominators.
  • Comparisons must preserve definitions and populations.
