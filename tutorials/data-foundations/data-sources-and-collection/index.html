Data Sources and Collection Methods

Learn where analytical data comes from, how collection choices shape meaning, and why provenance must be documented before analysis.

◷ 45 minBeginner

What you will learn

  • Distinguish primary and secondary data.
  • Recognize common collection methods.
  • Evaluate fitness for purpose.
  • Document provenance and collection limitations.
01

Primary and secondary data

Primary data are collected directly for the current purpose, such as a designed survey or experiment. Secondary data already exist because they were collected for another operational, administrative, or research purpose. Secondary data can be valuable, but definitions and coverage may not match the new question.

02

Collection methods

Common methods include surveys, interviews, observation, experiments, sensors, transactions, application logs, forms, and administrative systems. Each method has different errors: people may not respond, sensors may drift, forms may allow invalid entries, and logs may record only users who consented or completed an action.

03

Fitness for purpose

Data are useful only relative to a question. Check who or what is covered, time period, unit, definitions, granularity, update process, missing groups, access conditions, and legal or ethical limits. “Large” data can still be unrepresentative or irrelevant.

04

Provenance

Provenance records where data came from, who collected or transformed it, when, under which rules, and what changed. Without provenance, analysts cannot reproduce results or judge whether a dataset is appropriate.

Vocabulary

Key terms

Primary data

Data collected specifically for the current investigation.

Secondary data

Existing data reused for a new purpose.

Provenance

The documented origin and processing history of data.

Coverage

The people, events, places, and periods represented by a source.

Example

Choosing data for a school attendance study

The question is whether transport disruption reduced attendance in two school centers.

  1. 1

    Attendance registers are secondary administrative data.

  2. 2

    Transport disruption dates may come from external records.

  3. 3

    Check whether attendance was recorded consistently in both centers.

  4. 4

    Confirm that absences have dates and student identifiers.

  5. 5

    Document holidays and center closures as competing explanations.

Conclusion

The source can support the question only if dates, coverage, and recording rules are sufficiently consistent.

Summary

What to remember

  • Collection method affects meaning and error.
  • Secondary data must be checked against the new purpose.
  • Provenance supports trust and reproducibility.
  • More data is not automatically better data.
