01

From complaint to analysis

A manager says, “Sales are bad.” That is a concern, not yet an analytical question.

  1. Clarify what “bad” means: revenue, units, margin, or target attainment.
  2. Define the period and comparison: this month versus last month or budget.
  3. Identify dimensions: region, channel, product, and salesperson.
  4. Check whether returns, discounts, or missing transactions distort the result.
  5. Report the pattern and practical consequence.

A better question is: “Which regions and product categories explain the revenue gap against target in July, after returns?”

02

Seven days of retail sales

Daily sales in BDT: 12,000; 15,500; 11,800; 18,000; 16,200; 13,400; 21,000.

  1. The seven numbers are data values.
  2. Their unit is BDT and the entity is a day.
  3. The average, minimum, maximum, and chart are descriptive summaries.
  4. Using these seven days to predict every future day requires assumptions and uncertainty.

The observed average is about BDT 15,414; it describes these seven days, not a guaranteed future daily sale.

03

Customer-order table

A table contains order_id, order_date, customer_id, city, quantity, and revenue.

  1. Choose “order” as the unit of observation.
  2. Each row should represent one order.
  3. order_id is the identifier.
  4. city is a categorical variable; quantity and revenue are numerical variables.
  5. The value 3 under quantity belongs to one particular order.

Clear row meaning prevents accidental double counting and invalid aggregation.

04

Repairing a spreadsheet export

A sales sheet has a title in row 1, blank rows, merged headers, monthly columns, and a Total row.

  1. Remove presentation-only rows from the analytical table.
  2. Create one header row with unique names.
  3. Keep one observation per row.
  4. Move monthly column names into a month variable when repeated analysis is needed.
  5. Calculate totals in analysis rather than storing subtotal rows inside raw data.

A clean table is easier to validate, combine, filter, and reuse across Excel, SQL, Power BI, and Python.

05

Classifying an HR dataset

Fields: employee_id, department, age, monthly_salary, performance_rating, resigned.

  1. employee_id is a categorical identifier.
  2. department and resigned are categorical.
  3. age and monthly_salary are numerical.
  4. performance_rating is usually ordinal categorical unless a validated numerical scale justifies arithmetic.

Correct typing prevents invalid summaries such as the average employee ID or an unjustified mean of category codes.

06

Delivery operations

A courier records packages per rider, delivery duration, distance, and customer rating.

  1. packages is a discrete count.
  2. duration and distance are continuous measurements.
  3. rating 1–5 is ordinal categorical, not continuous.
  4. If duration is rounded to whole minutes, the recorded values look discrete but the underlying concept remains continuous.

Classification should reflect how the quantity is generated and used.

07

Four variables, four scales

Analyze payment_method, service_priority, temperature_c, and order_value.

  1. payment_method is nominal.
  2. service_priority (low, medium, high) is ordinal.
  3. temperature_c is interval.
  4. order_value is ratio because zero means no monetary value and ratios are interpretable.

The scale determines which comparisons are logically defensible.

Website activity data

09

Choosing data for a school attendance study

The question is whether transport disruption reduced attendance in two school centers.

  1. Attendance registers are secondary administrative data.
  2. Transport disruption dates may come from external records.
  3. Check whether attendance was recorded consistently in both centers.
  4. Confirm that absences have dates and student identifiers.
  5. Document holidays and center closures as competing explanations.

The source can support the question only if dates, coverage, and recording rules are sufficiently consistent.

10

Employee satisfaction survey

An organization has 500 eligible employees and surveys 120 selected employees.

  1. The 500 eligible employees are the target population.
  2. The 120 respondents form the observed sample if all selected responded.
  3. The true average satisfaction of all 500 is a parameter.
  4. The average among the 120 is a statistic.
  5. Nonresponse or poor selection can make the statistic biased, beyond ordinary sampling variation.

A statistic estimates a parameter only under a defensible sampling and measurement process.

11

Surveying two education centers

A program wants student satisfaction estimates and must represent both centers and grade levels.

  1. Define the eligible student population.
  2. Create strata by center and grade.
  3. Randomly sample within each stratum.
  4. Track selected, contacted, and responding students.
  5. Weight or qualify results if sampling fractions or response differ substantially.

Stratification protects representation of important subgroups but does not remove measurement or nonresponse error.

12

Training and employee performance

Employees who attended optional training have higher performance scores.

  1. The association is descriptive.
  2. Training attendance was not randomly assigned.
  3. More motivated or experienced employees may be more likely to attend.
  4. Manager support may affect both attendance and performance.
  5. Compare baseline performance, role, experience, and assignment process before causal claims.

The data support an association, not proof that training caused the difference.

Auditing a retail sales file

A CSV has 240 sales rows with order dates, quantities, prices, regions, and revenue.

  1. Measure missingness for required fields.
  2. Validate dates, positive quantities, allowed regions, and revenue arithmetic.
  3. Check duplicate order-line keys.

  5. Report quality findings separately from business findings.

A transparent quality report tells users what was checked, what failed, and how findings affect interpretation.

Cleaning order records responsibly

The file has blank customer ages, repeated order IDs, a negative quantity, and one exceptionally large order.

  2. Use order_id plus line_id to confirm true duplicates.

16

Order value distribution

Order values: 450, 620, 780, 820, 1,050, 1,200, 1,450, 1,900, 2,400, 5,500.

  1. Choose intervals related to business use: below 1,000; 1,000–1,999; 2,000–2,999; 3,000 or more.
  2. Count orders in each interval.
  3. Divide by 10 for relative frequencies.
  4. Notice that the last bin contains a high-value order but hides its exact magnitude.
  5. Inspect the raw maximum alongside the grouped table.

The table communicates distribution compactly, but it should not replace examination of unusual values.

17

Defining an on-time delivery rate

Operations wants to know whether delivery performance improved.

  1. Define eligible delivered orders and exclude cancelled orders.
  2. Define on-time: delivered on or before promised timestamp.
  3. Numerator: eligible orders delivered on time.
  4. Denominator: all eligible delivered orders.
  5. Specify timezone, period, order grain, data source, and comparison period.

The metric can now be reproduced and audited instead of interpreted differently by each team.

EDA of retail sales

The retail dataset contains order date, region, category, quantity, revenue, discount, and profit.

  1. Confirm row grain and date coverage.
  2. Profile missing, invalid, and duplicate records.
  3. Summarize revenue, profit, quantity, and discount distributions.
  4. Compare categories and regions using counts, totals, medians, and rates.

  6. Write findings, limitations, and next questions.

20

Publishing a healthcare dashboard

A public dashboard would show monthly visits by area, age group, diagnosis category, and clinic.

  1. Confirm public reporting purpose and legal basis.
  2. Remove unnecessary fields and use aggregated groups.
  3. Assess small cells and combinations that may identify individuals.
  4. Apply access, suppression, and retention controls.
  5. Review subgroup fairness and stigmatizing labels.
  6. Publish definitions, coverage, and limitations.

Responsible publication balances usefulness with privacy, fairness, and the risk of harm.

Suggested project workflow

Use the included retail_sales.csv and data dictionary.

  1. Read the project brief and write three analytical questions.
  2. Inspect the data dictionary and confirm one-row meaning.
  3. Profile structure and quality before calculating KPIs.
  4. Create summary tables and one trend chart.
  5. Investigate at least one unusual value.
  6. Write findings with units, time period, and limitations.
  7. Save a README and change log.

The project is complete when another learner can understand your question, repeat the audit, and trace each conclusion to evidence.
